                                    Abstract
Long-term conversational systems can recall past exchanges, but they may con-
fuse speakers, attribute one speaker’s statement to another persona, or make
personal claims without adequate evidence. MIRAI addresses these issues with
an evidence-scoped identity ledger. The ledger combines the ownership of sources,
time-in-valid records, update by version, recipient-sensitive style rules and a
response level claim check. We tested the system using a controlled benchmark of
40 test personas, 1,600 identity items, 400 no-evidence trials, 240 identity-update
trials, and 800 source-attribution trials. More challenging conditions and wording
were tested with an additional 20 personas. The full configuration returned the
expected identity value for 1,417 of 1,600 items and the correct updated value for
216 of 240 update trials. Abstention accuracy was 0.893, source-attribution accu-
racy was 0.948, and style-constraint satisfaction was 0.919. The entire system
scored higher on the benchmark-specific item measure in the matched compar-
isons compared than other versions that did not have the ownership-and-consent
gate or the version ledger. The descriptive results also revealed that recipient-
sensitive style had its most evident influence on style compliance and claim
checking on abstention from unsupported claims.

Keywords: conversational memory, identity continuity, persona benchmark,
provenance, versioned updates

1 Introduction
A conversational agent may also make the incorrect response even on a statement that
is recalled by a long-term, conversational agent. This may occur when the statement
was made by some other speaker, relates to a preference that has become obsolete
or when it was not allowed to be incorporated in the persona profile. MIRAI brings
these differences to the fore. Identity continuity in this work is consistent with the
record that exists, in real-time; this does not imply that the system represents the
consciousness or judgment of a real person.
    The previous conversations are more readily available with persistent memory.
Even retrieval is, however, insufficient to determine the ownership of a statement, or
whether the fact itself is up to date, or the adequacy of the evidence to a personal
claim. MIRAI puts these decisions within the memory pipeline, rather than putting
them completely within the response model.
    We explore how an evidence-scoped, versioned identity pipeline can minimize the
unsupported and false claims of identity. We also quantify its influence on accuracy
on items, on abstention, on identity updates, on source attribution, and style sensitive
to the recipient. In all the comparisons, the response model, corpus, prompt, budget
of retrieval, and the settings of decoding were held constant. Clearly defined con-
trol bundles, ownership-and-consent gate and the version ledger were tested whereas
recipient-sensitive style, and claim checking were discarded one at a time.
    It is not just another memory store, but an identity-continuity pathway which can
be audited. Sources of a claim, the duration of validity of the claim, the version of the
active identity, the policy of style used with the intended recipient, and the evidence
used to support each individual claim is recorded in MIRAI among the final response.
The benchmark looks at the failure at all these decision points as opposed to a single
score of monitoring memory.
    As a smart information system, MIRAI relates a generative response model to a
record layer that organizes knowledge and is changed. The provenance, consent, and
valid time are explicitly stored as database fields rather than being smashed down
into an untyped index of conversations. Supersession and recipient policy are also
explicitly stored as database fields. The design thus also facilitates intelligent retrieval
and generation of answers while keeping the link between stored evidence and the
final answer traceable and open to inspection. A potential use case is a conversational
service (with multiple users) in which preferences and personal facts are dynamic,
multiple speakers make statements, and the allowed answer form is dependent on who
is receiving the answer.
    The study has four practical questions guiding it. First, does the ownership-and-
consent gate help to minimize source attribution errors? Second, is the valid-time
version better in providing the answers following an identity update? Third, are
recipient-specific rules better than no rules as far as adherence to observable style
requirements? Lastly, does checking by response level of claims help the system abstain
from answering in cases where the system lacks evidence? We take the questions one
at a time since a single score in retrieval cannot provide information as to where an
identity-continuity pipeline is working or not.

2 Related work and research gap
Table 1 compares MIRAI with the main approaches to memory, temporal reasoning,
provenance, and evaluation considered in this study.
    MemGPT controls context capacity, using a hierarchical memory structure [13].
Mem0 focuses on the scalable extraction, consolidation, and retrieval of long-term
memories [2]. For evaluation, LoCoMo tests recall and reasoning in very long con-
versations [6], whereas LongMemEval includes information extraction, multi-session,
and temporal reasoning, knowledge updates, and abstention [17]. TReMu and THEA-
NINE add time-aware reasoning and temporal memory models [4, 7]. Collaborative
Memory provides provenance-sensitive sharing and dynamic access control [15]. Strat-
ified Context Reconstruction isolates governing facts and episodic memories and uses
temporally stratified retrieval to resolve superseded facts [1]. In other recent papers,
long-term dialogue memory is defined in terms of participant-specific source monitor-
ing [16] and through distinctions between operational recall in language models and
biological memory [14]. Persistence, retrieval, temporal updating, provenance, typed
retrieval and access control have already been defined in this body of work. MIRAI
differs in how it combines and evaluates these controls.




                                             3
    The individual capabilities are therefore not new. The changing conversational
events in TReMu and THEANINE are structured to support temporal reasoning.
Combining provenance with asymmetric access rules that may evolve over time is
the focus of Collaborative Memory, while distinguishing governing facts from episodic
memories and resolving superseded records is the focus of Stratified Context Recon-
struction. DPCA is more specific to the memory and source monitoring of participants,
as compared to Mem0, which is concerned with the extraction and consolidation
of memory. Long-horizon recall, updating, reasoning, and abstention are evaluated
by LoCoMo and LongMemEval. Accordingly, MIRAI does not claim that temporal
updating, typed retrieval, provenance, source monitoring, access control, or long-term
retrieval is individually new.
    MIRAI combines source ownership, consent state, versioned valid-time identity,
recipient-specific style policy, and response-level claim verification in a single shared
record contract. This contract governs the path from memory admission and retrieval
to verification of generated claims. None of the reviewed systems combines all these
controls in a single enforcement path and evaluates the corresponding boundaries
under the same response model, retrieval budget, and scoring procedure. Table 1 there-
fore compares mechanisms rather than published scores. It should not be interpreted
as a head-to-head ranking or a definitive claim of priority.

interval indicates when the claim is valid. Past records are stored in an append-only
ledger.
    The update gate verifies consent, source authority, attribute compatibility, and
event time before altering the active identity profile. A persona can make an authenti-
cated correction that supersedes a previous preference. By contrast, another speaker’s
statement remains dialogue evidence unless that speaker is authorized to update the
persona’s record. Conflicting or low-confidence records are retained for review and do
not automatically modify the active profile.

3.2 Retrieval, style control, and claim checking
Candidate evidence is ranked by

                  S (r, q ) = 0.45scos + 0.20slex + 0.15srec + 0.20strust ,              (2)

where all components are normalised to [0, 1]. The system applies consent, persona, and
validity filters before sending the eight highest-ranked records to the response model.
We fixed the weight profile using the eight development personas before evaluating the
primary and held-out sets. The highest weight (0.45) is given to semantic similarity,
followed by the individual weights of lexical (0.20), recency (0.15), and source trust
weights (0.20). The four coefficients are all greater than or equal to zero with the sum
of the coefficients equal to one. Sub-labels of the primary test and held-out test were
not utilized to manipulate the weights and the retrieval depth. Since the reported
experiment did not extend to sensitivity analyzing the rest of the weight profiles, these
coefficients are introduced as a fixed design option, and not as an optimum that has
been determined by itself.
    The first response model prompt includes the request by the user, the available evi-
dence record identifiers and valid time, the profile of the active identity, and the style
rules of the target recipient. After creating a draft, a claim checker divides personal
statements into atomic claims and cross-examines each one against the authorized
records. A rewrite is elicited when the claimed source conflicts with the record’s own-
ership, an identity value is stale or that a claim has no support. In case the rewritten
response is not successful, the system abstains from responding. To support auditing,
MIRAI stores the evidence retrieved, the version of identity, the decision on the draft
and the final response.
    These are controls that are used sequentially. The implementation initially chooses
records based on the persona requested and subsequently identifies consent, source
authority, status and valid time. The active version of identity is identified before
retrieval is scored, by means of supersession links. A list of the top eight eligible records
(along with their identifiers and their validity information) is provided along with the
active policy on the recipients. Individual claims of the generated answer are verified
with this same set of records. The system allows one constrained rewrite after a failed
check and refuses to respond in case the second check fails. This order prevents an
ineligible and overridden record from being counted as valid solely due to a high score
on retrieval.

3.3 Differentiated contribution
Long-term memory retrieval, persistent memory, version histories, and response
checking are not presented as individually new contributions. MIRAI’s architectural
contribution is the common record contract that binds them. The source actor, per-
mission state, validity span, and version recorded at memory admission are preserved
through retrieval and subsequently checked against atomic claims in the generated
response.
    The contract is enforced and evaluated at four boundaries: authorized writes, valid-
time selection, presentation to a specific recipient, and verification of claims against
evidence. The matched configurations remove either a control bundle or a downstream
component while keeping the model and retrieval budget constant. The resulting
changes can therefore indicate which boundary is associated with a performance differ-
ence. However, an ablation that removes a bundle cannot determine the contribution
of each field within that bundle.
    The resulting enforcement path is shown in Figure 1. Candidate memories must
pass ownership, consent, source-authority, and valid-time checks before they can mod-
ify the active identity profile. Records that pass the eligibility checks are then ranked
for the current query and combined with the current identity version and recipient
policy. Once an answer has been created, its personal claims are vetted with the
records.

3.4 Ablation configurations
The three functional baselines are matched implementations constructed for this study.
We do not claim that they reproduce MemGPT, Mem0, or any other published sys-
tem. Instead, they isolate architectural mechanisms while keeping the response model,
embedding model, retrieval depth, and decoding settings constant. This facilitates
controlled component comparisons but does not establish that MIRAI outperforms an
external system.

4 Methodology
4.1 Experimental settings
All major configurations used text-embedding-3-small for embeddings and
gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18 to generate responses. In 2025, work was conducted on
the main benchmark, development tuning and the GPT-4o mini response runs. The
automated checker audit was conducted in 2026 using the fixed evaluator snapshots
described in Section 4.3. We set the temperature to 0.2, top-p to 1.0, and the max-
imum output length to 512 tokens. Each query had up to 8 records that could be
retrieved. The experiments were performed using Python 3.11 on a Windows 11 work-
station with an Intel Core i7 processor and 32 GB of memory. Responses were obtained
through the OpenAI API; no local GPUs were used.
    The benchmark-generation seed was 20260619. The scoring procedure, query order,
prompts, and persona records were the same across all configurations. We used 10,000
persona-level cluster-bootstrap resamples to compute confidence intervals and 10,000
sign-flipping permutations.




                                              7
4.2 Benchmark construction and data splits
Retrieval and update parameters were tuned using eight development personas. None
appeared in the test sets or reported results. There were 40 personas (P001–P040)
in the main set of tests. Each persona had 10 personal facts, eight preferences, 10
dated events, six relationships and six values. This produced 40 items per persona and
1,600 item queries in total. Each persona also had 10 no-evidence trials, six identity-
update trials, and 20 source-attribution trials, for totals of 400, 240, and 800 trials.
We checked four style rules for each item response, producing 6,400 rule decisions.
    The held-out set contained 20 personas (H001–H020), 800 identity items, 200
no-evidence trials, 120 update trials and 400 source-attribution trials. These ques-
tions included Malay–English code-switching and paraphrases that were not used in
the primary test set. Both sets used the same exclusion, formatting, and scoring
procedures.
    The expected current value, known evidence owner, and explicit temporal rela-
tionship make errors traceable in the controlled design. However, the design does not
reproduce the ambiguity, conversational repair, or uneven evidence found in natural
interactions. The held-out set and public benchmarks test transfer but do not replace
naturally occurring longitudinal conversational data.

4.4 Statistical analysis
The unit of resampling was the persona. For each configuration and outcome, we
sampled 40 test personas with replacement 10,000 times. The 95% interval of the
recomputed scores was the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The seven major comparisons
took persona-level F1 difference between full MIRAI and all alternatives. To test the
two sided permutation tests with the paired sign flips, we applied 10,000 random sign
vectors and

4.5 Established systems and independent benchmarks
In the case of LoCoMo, we used all 1,986 question–answer items in the release of ten
conversations. The items included single-hop, multi-hop, temporal, open-domain, and
adversarial questions, and predictions were evaluated using the normalised partial-
match F1 procedure of the benchmark. For LongMemEval-S, we used all 500 questions
on information extraction, multi-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, knowledge
updates and abstention. Responses were scored post hoc using binary correctness.
    Both benchmark conversations were consumed in order of time. MIRAI used the
record schema, retrieval weights, top-eight budget and single-rewrite rule of the orig-
inal experiment. We generated one final response for each question and did not tune
prompts or weights using benchmark labels. These tests therefore check the transfer-
ability of the fixed configuration, but do not assess optimization of the configuration
to either of the benchmarks.
    The systems in Table 1 that are labeled as MemGPT, Mem0, etc., refer to related
work and do not label the corresponding matched baselines. Their published scores,
which we did not combine with the results of MIRAI, are not directly comparable
because of differences in response models, retrieval budgets, and evaluation procedures.
We therefore make no head-to-head performance claim against these systems.

Full MIRAI had 1,417 true positives, 99 false positives and 183 false negatives for a
micro-F1 of 0.909. The correct updated value was returned in 216 of 240 trials (0.900;
95% CI [0.863, 0.938]). The system also made the correct no-evidence rate of 357 of
400 (0.893), made the correct source of evidence rate of 758 of 800 (0.948) and passed
the 5,883 of 6,400 style checks (0.919).
    The descriptive contrasts on the primary set corresponded to the targeted control
boundaries. The difference in source-attribution accuracy between full MIRAI and the
configuration without the ownership-and-consent gate was 0.274 (0.948 versus 0.674).
Removing the version ledger reduced update accuracy by 0.225, from 0.900 to 0.675.
Style satisfaction was 0.919 for full MIRAI and 0.706 without recipient-sensitive style,
a difference of 0.213. The correct abstention rate on no-evidence trials was 0.893 for full
MIRAI versus 0.608 without claim checking (difference 0.285). These are descriptive
contrasts since the paired significance tests that were prespecified were restricted to
item-level micro-F1.
    In the case of the 320-response validation set, there was agreement between the
evaluator agents with 0.89 in claim labels and 0.92 in style-rule verdict. All the
disagreements were settled by the adjudication agent. Compared to these entirely auto-
mated reference labels, the claim processor had an accuracy of 0.94, recall 0.92 and
F1 0.93. The style checker had a 0.95 accuracy. In this audit, there is a quantification
of consensus on systematic assistance and clear guidelines; there is no evaluation of
the quality of responses as it is viewed by individuals.

6 Error analysis and computational overhead
6.1 Error analysis
We checked all of the full-MIRAI failures in the three targeted factual assessments.
The retrieved record versions, identity versions, draft answers as well as final answers
were used in the review. Based on the numbers of Table 4, the review covered 24 failed
identity-update trials, 42 incorrect source-attribution trials, and 43 failed no-evidence
trials. False positives and false negatives from the item queries were outside the scope
of this specific review. Three patterns recurred. First, an earlier preference was ranked
above a dated correction. Second, an event involving another speaker was attributed to
the target persona. Third, the system generated a plausible personalized claim despite
the absence of supporting evidence. Representative examples are given in Table 8.
    The former case shows that the need to retain previous versions is insufficient if

6.2 Computational overhead
Table 9 reports measurements from recorded end-to-end runs. Full MIRAI had higher
latency and input-token use because it included evidence filtering, version resolution,
and claim verification. We do not report monetary cost. The token summary does
not include every conditional rewrite or all embedding, retrieval, storage, and network
operations; it therefore cannot estimate total operational cost.

7 Discussion and limitations
Following Holm correction, persona-level item F1 on the primary set was significantly
higher for Full MIRAI than for the configurations without the ownership-and-
consent gate or the version ledger. It also performed much better than all three
matched memory baselines on this measure. The contrasts with the settings where
the relationship-sensitive style was off and when there was no claim checking were not
statistically significant. The corresponding targeted outcomes are therefore reported
descriptively. Eliminating relationship-sensitive style was associated with a 0.213 drop
in style compliance and eliminating the claim check was associated with a 0.285 drop
in correct abstention on no-evidence trials. The configuration without relationship-
sensitive style had a small item-F1 difference on the held-out set compared to full
MIRAI (0.883 vs. 0.879) although full MIRAI still had substantially higher style com-
pliance. The data thus do not imply that all components enhance all outcomes. Nor
can the ablations distinguish the contribution of each field within a gate.
    Traceable benchmark failures concern explicit evidence ownership, event time and
state of expected identity. The benchmark’s limited coverage, however, restricts the
external validity. It has a bounded number of attributes coded in its personas and
its code-switched queries are not as varied as those in naturally occurring Malaysian
conversations. The held-out set uses the same generator and scoring protocol but
changes the personas and wording. The outcomes of the public benchmarks broaden
the assessment, however, but do not establish how the system would perform in a
long-term deployment.


                                            13
    From an intelligent-information-systems perspective, the results support separating
an evolving, policy-bearing knowledge layer from the generative interface that uses it.
Changes in provenance and time, together with access conditions, are handled at the
database level by the record contract. The response layer accesses this information
through natural language. Storage, retrieval, updating of the data and verification of
the answers can be audited through this separation. This separation is also appropriate
for information systems that have to deal with evolving knowledge as opposed to
retrieving semantically close text.
    Deterministic checks can detect only the rules they implement. A response can
satisfy length, greeting, punctuation, and code-switching requirements without neces-
sarily being natural, useful, or trustworthy. The study outcomes do not measure these
qualities. The automated evaluator audit provides a second model-based check, but
does not provide an independent perceptual evaluation. The main ablations were con-
ducted with one GPT-4o mini snapshot, and cross-model tests are only done with full
MIRAI (GPT-4.1 mini). Moreover, the baselines are study-specific implementations
and not full replications of external systems. Any conclusions must thus be restricted
to factual support, temporal selection and source attribution, abstinence and explicit
style constraints within the experimental settings.

8 Conclusion
MIRAI links long-term memory and explicit ownership of sources, valid-time identity
records, recipient-sensitive style and response-level claim checking. The entire system
returned the expected value for 1,417 out of 1,600 identity items on the controlled
persona benchmark. Its source-attribution accuracy was 0.948, style-constraint satis-
faction was 0.919 and its update accuracy was 0.900. The ownership-and-consent gate
and the version ledger produced statistically significant differences on the primary
test’s item measure. Differences between recipient-sensitive style and claim checking
were largest for the targeted outcomes of style compliance and abstention. The inde-
pendent benchmark results and second response model results provide limited evidence
of transfer and the latency and token results show the observed computational over-
head. As an intelligent information system, MIRAI offers a clear contract between
the evolving database records, evidence-aware retrieval, and generated responses.
Broader claims will require evaluation with more response models and a wider range
of longitudinal multilingual dialogues.
